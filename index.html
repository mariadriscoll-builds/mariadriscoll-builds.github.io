Client adoption dashboard

Customer success operations

The problem. Customers were paying for a hardware-and-software product their teams were meant to use every working day. Whether they actually did was scattered across system summaries, automated report emails and portal logins, so nobody could see an account slipping until renewal.

What I built. A daily record, one file per day, answering seven fixed questions per account, and a dashboard drawn from it: one row per team in the field, one row for office activity. Field silent while the office keeps logging in points to a hardware fault. Both dark at once is a customer walking away. The publish step checks its own output before it goes live.

What changed. It replaced a hand-kept spreadsheet tab as the team's record of customer adoption, and a run of red squares became the cue to call an account.

Shows: adoption and account-health monitoring, data modelling, Python, data visualisation. The same approach works for any product that leaves a usage trail.

Client account records

Account management and data design

The problem. What the team knew about each account was spread across a spreadsheet, inboxes, call notes and people's heads. Promises made on calls slipped, and nobody could tell a fact confirmed last week from one that was months out of date.

What I built. One structured record per account, shown as a single page: the customer journey, training, equipment, commercial terms, contacts, and the two lists that matter most, what we owe them and what they owe us. Every key fact records where it came from and when it was last confirmed, and turns amber after 30 days. A unit is never marked working just because nobody reported it broken.

What changed. It replaced a hand-kept spreadsheet as the place to prepare for a client call, and the daily AI pass kept it current from the same sources as the dashboard.

Shows: account management, data modelling, information design, keeping records honest.

The morning run

AI workflow design

The problem. Signals about each client lived in call recordings, three mailboxes, calendars, team chat and automated reports. Promises made on calls slipped, and nobody had time to read everything.

What I built. Scheduled AI agents, run in sequence, that update every client's working file, raise new commitments as tasks, prepare meeting briefs and harvest customer quotes. Most of the work was the rules: close nothing without primary evidence, treat second-hand summaries as leads, and check a source is current before trusting a quiet result.

What changed. Each morning starts with current client files and a short list of what needs attention, and nothing is marked done without proof.

Shows: process design, writing precise instructions for AI agents, data governance, measuring what works.

Customer quote library

Marketing operations and AI

The problem. Customers said great things on calls, in emails and to the team in chat, and none of it was findable when someone needed a testimonial. Worse, paraphrases kept turning into "quotes".

What I built. A weekly AI harvest that reads new call records, chat and email, keeps only positive words from customer-side speakers, and files each one by theme with its source. Every quote is marked verbatim, relayed, paraphrased or unverified, and prospects and buying signals are kept apart from testimonials. Before reporting a quiet week, it checks that the call records were actually filed.

What changed. Marketing and sales can search a library of customer quotes and copy one with its attribution in seconds, knowing which ones are cleared to use in public.

Shows: voice-of-customer programmes, content operations, AI workflow design, editorial judgment.

Logins aren't usage

Analysis and a request to engineering

The problem. The product's activity screen was about to be used to judge which clients were engaged. It said no customer had the mobile app installed, while I was talking to customers who used it daily.

What I built. A short evidence report: five observations showing why the numbers couldn't be trusted, from mis-attributed accounts to a log that ignores exactly the traffic the app generates, and six prioritised asks for the developers.

What changed. The figures stayed out of client records and reviews, and the developers got a ranked list to work from instead of a vague complaint.

Shows: analytical scepticism, writing for engineers, turning a finding into a decision.

Website, calculators and lead capture

Marketing, built end to end

The problem. A startup's new website needed to turn readers into qualified leads, and its forms were getting hit by spam bots.

What I built. The marketing site itself, shipped and maintained across more than twenty releases with a change log. Gated ROI calculators that show a prospect what they're losing, and a small Cloudflare relay that blocks bots and hides the form addresses. It captures the lead first, then quietly records the prospect's own numbers once they stop adjusting, so sales sees real figures without sending anyone a second email.

Shows: web build, JavaScript, marketing automation, conversion design.

How I work

Start with the people whose behaviour has to change. Public health taught me that a good product or message isn't enough. Adoption happens when the new way fits how people already work, so that's where I start.

Diagnose before prescribing. Most problems I'm asked to fix turn out to be a symptom of something else. I find the root cause before I build anything.

A quiet result needs checking before it's believed. "Nothing new this week" sometimes means nothing happened, and sometimes means nobody filed the data. I check which before I report it.

Silence is not completion. A task is done when there's evidence it happened, not when it stops coming up.

Fix the rules, not the output. When a report or an agent gets something wrong, I change the instruction or the template that produced it, so it doesn't happen again next week.
